01 — NDP

National Day, and why it falls in August

Singapore became an independent, sovereign nation on 9 August 1965, separating from Malaysia after a short-lived merger. Every year since, 9 August has been marked as National Day, and 1966, the first anniversary, is where the timeline on this page begins.

The National Day Parade (NDP) is the centrepiece: a live, nationally televised show combining military and civilian marching contingents, a fighter-jet flypast, mass dance and music performances, and a fireworks finale, usually built around a different creative theme each year. It has been held annually with the venue, format and songs all changing considerably over six decades, which is what the rest of this page tracks.

02 — Venues

Three main locations

Padang is simply Malay for "field"; a generic word used all over Singapore and Malaysia for an open town green. The Padang, fronting City Hall and the old Supreme Court, is specific. It's where independence was formally marked in 1965, which is why NDP keeps returning to it. It has hosted on a fixed cycle since 1984 (every 3 years, then every 5 from 1995); most other years defaulted the old National Stadium until 2006, then The Float at Marina Bay from 2007.

Parade venue by year, 1966–2026

A handful of early years spread the parade across many venues at once instead of one central site: 1975 (the 10th anniversary) used 13 simultaneous venues; 1981 used both Jurong and Queenstown Stadiums; 1977, 1979 and 1983 were also decentralised, though their specific sites aren't documented in the sources used here. The general idea was to bring the celebration out of the city centre and into more neighbourhoods. 2016 broke the regular cycle for the new National Stadium's debut; 2019 added an extra Padang year for the bicentennial; 2023–25 sat at the Padang while The Float's replacement was under construction. 2026 returns to the National Stadium for the first time in a decade.

03 — Songs

A song a year, mostly

No song joined the parade until 1984. The first song, Stand Up for Singapore, was written for the 25th anniversary of self-government. A milestone worth marking even though full independence was still six years away in 1965 terms. The bar height counts how many songs each year had, taller means more songs, the colour depicts the language.

Songs per year, coloured by language

From 1998 to 2008, an eleven-year run, releasing an English and a Mandarin bilingual version side by side was close to routine. Outside that window songs were English only. Malay and Tamil versions are effectively absent, despite both being official languages.

A traditional for a song each year began until 2014. No new one was commissioned, after several years of lukewarm reception to new songs, and the parade instead revived three old favourites as a medley — One People, One Nation, One Singapore (1990), Home (1998) and What Do You See? (2009).

04 — People

Composers, lyricists, performers, and the overlap

Three different jobs, often done by different people: the composer writes the music, the lyricist writes the words, and the performer is who actually sang it for that year's parade. 05 — Slogans

Back to the timeline, when official slogans began

Not every year has a documented slogan or theme. NDP's first documented official slogan, a short publicity catchphrase, "Our Singapore, Our Future." appeared in 1997. A separate, more formal "theme", usually a fuller sentence naming that year's milestone or narrative, starts showing up distinctly in sources from 2007. In practice, the two are often used loosely and interchangeably across different sources; only 2005 clearly documents both as distinct things at once ("The Future Is Ours To Make" as the slogan, and40 Years of Nation Building" as the theme).

Why this exists

I remember singing happily along to National Day songs, buying a cassette and memorising the lyrics from the small folded sheet inside the case.

On my children’s first National Day living in Singapore, what feels nostalgic and completely normal to me landed as something a little overwhelming and odd for them, right down to a school assignment asking them to listen to National Day Parade (NDP) songs and answer questions about them.

It got me wondering how the songs I grew up with compare with the ones Singapore sings today. What has changed? What has stayed? And what can the songs, their titles and the world around them tell us about Singapore over time?

I originally wanted to explore the lyrics themselves. But rather than reproduce or text-mine copyrighted lyrics, I decided to build this around information that is openly documented: song titles, languages, composers, performers, official NDP themes, parade venues and other published details.

The result is a small data story built mostly out of curiosity; a way to look at a familiar national tradition from a different angle, and hopefully something my kids, other Singaporeans and newcomers to Singapore can explore and make sense of too.

It’s a work in progress. If you spot something I’ve missed or find a pattern I haven’t, I’d love to hear about it.
